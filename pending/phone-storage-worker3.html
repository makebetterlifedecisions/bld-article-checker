Our practical starting point

Consider 256GB, then check your own usage

128GB can suit lighter use. Consider 512GB for growing local libraries and 1TB or more only when your recording, gaming or offline workflow justifies it.

Phone storage is easy to underestimate because the number on the box is not the amount you can freely use, and the files that consume it are no longer limited to photos and a few applications. High-resolution video, downloaded streaming content, large games, message attachments and offline maps can quietly fill a phone that seemed spacious on purchase day.

Buying too little storage can leave you constantly deleting files or paying for cloud storage. Buying far more than you will use can add hundreds of dollars to the price without improving speed, battery life or camera quality. The aim is to choose enough capacity for the entire time you expect to own the phone, with room left for updates and unexpected use.

This guide explains what 64GB, 128GB, 256GB, 512GB and 1TB mean in practical terms, who each capacity suits and how Australian buyers can estimate their real requirement before ordering.

The short answer

For everyday use, 256GB is our practical starting recommendation when your usage estimate supports it. It provides comfortable room for applications, photos, videos, messages and offline downloads without forcing most buyers into the highest-priced version.

  • 64GB: Avoid for a main phone unless your use is extremely light and you are comfortable managing cloud storage regularly.
  • 128GB: Enough for light users who stream most media, record little video and actively manage photos and downloads.
  • 256GB: Best for most buyers and the capacity we recommend when you are uncertain.
  • 512GB: Best for frequent photographers, 4K video users, mobile gamers, travellers and people keeping phones for several years.
  • 1TB or more: Mainly for creators, professionals, very large game libraries and people who need substantial content available offline.

Before deciding, check how much storage your current phone is using. Estimate growth across your planned ownership period, allow working space, then move to the next available capacity if the result sits close to the limit. A fixed percentage cannot predict every buyer's needs.

Storage is not the same as memory

Phone specifications often list storage and memory beside each other, but they do different jobs.

Storage holds applications, photos, videos, music, messages, downloads and system files when the phone is switched off. It is normally advertised as 128GB, 256GB, 512GB or 1TB.

Memory, usually called RAM, temporarily holds active applications and tasks. More RAM can help multitasking, but it does not give you more permanent room for photos or video. A phone advertised with 12GB of memory and 256GB of storage has 256GB for its operating system and your saved content, not 268GB.

Why the advertised capacity is not fully available

A new 256GB phone does not arrive with 256GB free. The operating system, preinstalled applications, recovery files and formatting consume part of the advertised capacity. The exact available amount varies by model, software version and region; check the selected configuration rather than relying on its headline capacity.

The system may also reserve working space for temporary files, application caches, software updates and background processing. That space is not wasted: the phone needs it to operate reliably. It does mean you should never plan to use every advertised gigabyte.

A nearly full phone can become frustrating even before it reaches zero. Camera recording may stop, applications may fail to update, downloads may be rejected and a major operating-system update may need temporary installation room. Keeping roughly 15 to 20 per cent free is a sensible practical target, not a technical rule that applies identically to every model.

Check what you use now

Your current phone gives a better answer than a generic online calculator. Check both the total used and which categories are responsible.

On an iPhone

Open Settings, General, then iPhone Storage. iOS displays used and available capacity, recommendations and a category breakdown. Tap an application to see how much is the application itself and how much is its documents and data.

On a Samsung Galaxy

Open Settings, Device care, then Storage. Menu wording can vary with the One UI version. Review images, video, audio, documents, applications, installation files and the recycle bin rather than looking only at the headline figure.

On a Google Pixel or another Android phone

Open Settings, Storage. The Files by Google application can also identify large files, duplicates, downloads and content that has already been backed up.

Do this before cleaning the phone. You want to see your natural use, not an artificially low number created five minutes before making the decision. If your current 128GB phone is using 105GB after two years, buying another 128GB model for the next four years is unlikely to end well.

A simple storage calculation

Use this method when moving to a new phone:

  1. Record current used storage and identify how much is system data versus apps and files you will keep.
  2. Remove files you genuinely will not transfer, such as old downloads or duplicate video.
  3. Add expected growth for the years you plan to keep the next phone.
  4. Allow for the new phone's system storage once, then leave free working space. Roughly 15 to 20 per cent is a planning preference, not a manufacturer requirement.
  5. Choose the next available capacity if your estimate is close to the limit.

Suppose you will keep 80GB of apps and personal files, excluding system storage. At an illustrative 20GB of growth each year for four years, that becomes 160GB of data. Add the new phone's system needs and free working space. A 256GB model may provide useful headroom; 128GB cannot hold that data estimate. If your starting figure already includes the operating system, separate it out or adjust it for the new phone rather than adding the same allowance twice.

Do not assume usage will stay flat. New cameras create larger files, games receive bigger content packs, family group chats accumulate video and offline streaming becomes more tempting before travel.

Is 64GB enough?

Only for very light or specialised use.

A 64GB phone may work for calls, messages, banking, navigation and a small application collection when photos are optimised through the cloud. It can also make sense as a secondary work phone, emergency phone or device for someone who rarely takes photos and streams rather than downloads entertainment.

For a new main phone, however, 64GB leaves limited breathing room after the operating system and applications. A few large games, years of message attachments or several hours of downloaded video can consume the remaining space surprisingly quickly.

Choose 64GB only when the price is extremely low, the use is predictable and the owner is willing to manage storage. It is a poor choice for children who install games freely, travellers who download media or anyone hoping to keep the device for several years.

Is 128GB enough?

Yes for light users, but it is no longer the automatic safe choice.

128GB can suit someone who:

  • Streams music and video instead of keeping large offline libraries.
  • Takes ordinary photos but records relatively little 4K video.
  • Uses a modest collection of social, banking, shopping and communication applications.
  • Backs up photos and is comfortable removing local originals.
  • Replaces the phone every two or three years.

It becomes restrictive for buyers with large game libraries, constant family videos, extensive WhatsApp or Messenger histories, lossless music downloads or unreliable internet access. It is also less attractive when the phone has no microSD slot.

If you already use more than about 80GB, think carefully before buying 128GB. The remaining room will be smaller than it appears once your transferred data, the new operating system and future growth are included.

Is 256GB enough?

Yes for most people, which is why it is our default recommendation.

256GB accommodates a broad application collection, years of ordinary photos, a useful amount of 4K video, several large games and offline music or streaming downloads. It also provides enough headroom to reduce constant storage management.

The iPhone 17 and Galaxy S26 provide examples of 256GB and 512GB choices in Australia. Availability can vary by colour, seller and configuration. Compare capacities within the phone model you actually want.

256GB is particularly sensible if you plan to keep the phone for four or five years. It does not guarantee unlimited room, especially for heavy video users, but it avoids the most common capacity regret without paying for professional-level storage.

Who should buy 512GB?

Buy 512GB when storage is part of how you use the phone, not merely something happening in the background.

It is worthwhile for:

  • Parents who record large amounts of family video.
  • Travellers who download movies, television, music, maps and documents before leaving reliable internet.
  • Mobile gamers who keep several large titles installed.
  • Users who shoot high-resolution, RAW, slow-motion or 4K video.
  • People carrying a large lossless music library.
  • Owners who dislike deleting content or depending on cloud access.
  • Buyers planning to keep the phone for five years or longer.

It may also be the better value when a launch offer doubles storage at no additional charge. Compare the real transaction price, not only the normal price difference between capacities.

Do not buy 512GB merely because it sounds futureproof. If your current phone has accumulated only 55GB after three years and your habits are stable, most of that extra space will probably remain empty.

Who needs 1TB or more?

Very few ordinary users need 1TB.

It is designed for people who use a phone as a serious production or storage device: professional video creators, mobile filmmakers, photographers keeping RAW files, musicians carrying extensive high-quality audio, field workers operating without dependable internet and gamers maintaining unusually large libraries.

Google's Pixel 10 Pro is available with capacities up to 1TB, while the iPhone 17 Pro reaches 1TB and the iPhone 17 Pro Max offers up to 2TB. Those options exist because advanced recording formats and professional workflows can create enormous files, not because ordinary buyers suddenly need a terabyte for calls and social media.

At this level, compare the storage upgrade price with alternative equipment. A creator may obtain better value from a lower-capacity phone plus an external USB-C drive and a disciplined backup workflow. Confirm that the phone, recording application and drive support the exact workflow before relying on it.

Photos do not usually cause the biggest surprise

People often blame photos for storage problems, but video usually grows faster. Photo sizes vary with resolution, format, detail, editing features and whether RAW capture is enabled. Thousands of efficient HEIF or JPEG photos can fit comfortably, while RAW images occupy much more space.

Live Photos and motion-photo features store additional movement around the still image. Burst shooting creates many frames. Editing applications may retain originals, exports and cached previews. A library that appears to contain one version of each image can therefore use more storage than expected.

Regularly remove accidental screenshots, failed bursts and duplicate exports, but do not treat cloud backup as permission to delete blindly. Verify that important images have completed uploading and can be accessed from another device first.

Video changes the calculation

Video storage varies dramatically with resolution, frame rate, compression, high-dynamic-range settings and recording format. Recording at 4K and 60 frames per second generally consumes far more capacity than 1080p at 30 frames per second. Professional formats can use dramatically more again.

If you record children's sport, concerts, holidays or long family events, 512GB can be justified even when the rest of your use is moderate. The same applies to creators who edit on the phone because the original clips, application cache and exported version can coexist temporarily.

Use the camera's estimated recording time as a guide and run a one-minute test using your preferred settings. Check the resulting file size, multiply it by your typical recording time and include room for editing. This produces a more reliable estimate than a generic figure from another phone.

Applications and games keep growing

The download size shown in an app store is not always its final footprint. Applications can add documents, caches, offline data, language packs and updates. Social applications store viewed media, browsers retain website data and navigation applications download maps.

Large mobile games may require substantial additional data after installation. Updates can temporarily need both the old and new files, and optional high-resolution texture packs increase the total. A gaming phone with 128GB may feel constrained after only a few major titles.

Check the storage used by the games you actually play. If a phone is replacing part of your console or handheld gaming, treat 256GB as the practical minimum and consider 512GB.

Messages, social media and downloads

Years of conversations can contain far more data than the text suggests. Group chats accumulate photos, memes, voice messages, documents and video. Messaging applications may save media both inside the application and again in the photo library, creating apparent duplicates.

Streaming applications also create hidden pressure when automatic downloads are enabled. Music playlists, podcast backlogs, television episodes and offline video remain stored until removed. A few forgotten seasons can occupy more space than thousands of ordinary documents.

Review each application's own storage manager before deleting the whole application. Remove large attachments and expired downloads while preserving conversations or content that matters.

Does cloud storage replace phone storage?

No. Cloud storage can reduce local pressure, but it does not replace adequate device storage.

Apple provides 5GB of free iCloud storage. Australian iCloud+ plans include 50GB, 200GB, 2TB, 6TB and 12TB options; check Apple’s current Australian monthly pricing before choosing a plan. With iCloud Photos and Optimise Storage enabled, full-resolution originals remain in iCloud while smaller versions can be kept on the iPhone.

Google Accounts include 15GB shared across Google Photos, Drive and Gmail. Google One expands that allowance, with consumer plans including 100GB and 200GB options and larger AI-linked tiers. Plans and pricing can change, so confirm the Australian checkout price before subscribing.

Cloud optimisation has limitations:

  • You need a reliable internet connection to retrieve offloaded originals.
  • Uploading large libraries can consume time and data.
  • A subscription becomes an ongoing ownership cost.
  • Cloud storage can fill independently of the phone.
  • Deleting a synchronised photo may delete it across connected devices.
  • Backup and synchronisation are not always the same thing.

Cloud services can be useful for protection, access and convenience, but synchronisation alone is not a separate backup. It is a poor excuse for buying a phone that is already too small for your normal offline use.

Can you add a microSD card later?

Most premium phones no longer provide microSD expansion. Current iPhones, Galaxy S flagships and Google Pixel flagships rely on fixed internal storage. Some budget, rugged and specialist Android phones still support cards; Samsung's Galaxy XCover7 Pro, for example, supports microSD cards up to 2TB.

Even when a phone accepts a card, it may store photos, video and documents there but keep applications or important system data internally. Card performance, reliability and supported capacity also vary. Confirm the exact Australian model specification rather than assuming every phone from the same brand includes expansion.

If there is no microSD slot, the capacity chosen at checkout is permanent. USB-C drives can transfer or archive files, but they are not as seamless as having sufficient internal storage every day.

How long will you keep the phone?

Storage should be matched to ownership length. A buyer replacing a phone in two years can make a tighter capacity work. Someone keeping it for five or six years needs room for larger applications, more photos, new features and repeated software updates.

Long software-support promises make this more important. A phone may remain secure and supported long after its storage has become annoying. Paying for 256GB instead of 128GB can be worthwhile if it prevents an otherwise unnecessary early replacement.

Storage does not directly make a phone faster, but adequate free space helps avoid update failures and constant cleanup. Capacity is therefore part of practical longevity even though it is not a performance specification.

Storage recommendations by user type

Basic calls, messages and banking

Recommended: 128GB. A carefully managed 64GB phone can work, but 128GB provides a much more comfortable margin and should last longer.

Typical family and everyday use

Recommended: 256GB. This suits photos, short videos, social applications, navigation, music, banking and a reasonable collection of downloads without constant management.

Teenagers and mobile gamers

Recommended: 256GB minimum; consider 512GB. Games, social-media caches, saved video and changing application habits make 128GB risky for enthusiastic users.

Parents recording children and family events

Recommended: 256GB or 512GB. Choose 512GB if long 4K recordings are common or if deleting family memories from the device creates anxiety.

Frequent travellers

Recommended: 256GB or 512GB. Offline maps, boarding documents, music, movies and photo backups require local space when mobile reception and hotel Wi-Fi cannot be trusted.

Content creators and professional video users

Recommended: 512GB to 1TB or more. Match the capacity to the chosen recording format, editing workflow and external-backup routine. Run real recording tests before purchasing.

When paying for more storage is worth it

Upgrade the capacity when:

  • Your current phone already uses more than two-thirds of the smaller option.
  • You plan to keep the new phone for at least four years.
  • The model has no expandable storage.
  • You record frequent 4K, RAW or professional-format content.
  • You keep large games, music or video available offline.
  • Your work depends on having files available without internet access.
  • A promotion provides the larger capacity at little or no extra cost.

When the storage upgrade is a waste

Keep the smaller option when your current use is low, your habits are stable and the upgrade price is disproportionate. Spending $300 or $400 for unused storage is not sensible futureproofing if that money could buy a better camera, longer battery life, a protective case or simply remain in your pocket.

Also compare the upgrade with several years of cloud cost. Cloud services can provide backup or synchronisation and access across devices, while internal storage provides fast offline capacity. They are not identical, but the comparison reveals which benefit you are actually paying for.

How to free space without losing important files

  1. Back up important photos, videos and documents, then verify the backup from another device.
  2. Delete failed videos, duplicate exports, screenshots and accidental bursts.
  3. Remove watched movies, old podcast episodes and unused offline playlists.
  4. Use the storage manager inside messaging applications to find large attachments.
  5. Offload or uninstall applications you no longer use.
  6. Clear application downloads and caches through supported settings rather than random cleaner applications.
  7. Empty recently deleted folders and recycle bins only after checking their contents.
  8. Restart the phone and review storage again after major cleanup.

Avoid cleaner applications promising dramatic speed improvements. Some provide little benefit, display aggressive advertising or request unnecessary permissions. The storage tools built into iOS, Android and trusted applications are normally sufficient.

Frequently asked questions

Is 128GB enough for a phone in 2026?

It is enough for light, cloud-first users who stream media, record little 4K video and manage downloads. It is not our default recommendation for heavy users or people keeping a phone for many years.

Is 256GB overkill?

Not necessarily. For many everyday buyers, 256GB is a comfortable middle ground. It provides room for normal growth without the high cost of 512GB or 1TB.

Does more storage make a phone faster?

Not automatically. Storage capacity and storage performance are separate specifications. However, leaving adequate free space can prevent update problems and reduce the disruption caused by constant cleanup.

Should I buy more storage or pay for cloud storage?

Choose internal storage for reliable offline access and cloud storage for backup, synchronisation and access across devices. Most people benefit from a sensible amount of both rather than relying entirely on one.

Can I upgrade phone storage later?

Usually not. Internal storage is fixed on current iPhones, Galaxy S phones and Pixel flagships. Some Android models accept microSD cards, but support must be confirmed before purchase.

How much free space should I keep?

Aiming to keep approximately 15 to 20 per cent free is sensible for downloads, temporary files and updates. The phone may still work with less, but regularly operating at the limit creates unnecessary problems.

Final verdict

Choose 256GB if you are unsure. It is a useful starting point for mixed everyday use, provided your estimate leaves room for future growth and system needs.

Choose 128GB only when your current use proves that it is enough and you are comfortable using cloud optimisation. Move to 512GB when video, gaming, travel or long ownership will materially increase local storage. Reserve 1TB and larger capacities for demanding professional or offline workflows.

The best answer is already on your current phone: check what you genuinely use, estimate how it will grow and leave breathing room. That approach is more reliable than buying the cheapest capacity or paying for an impressive number you will never fill.
