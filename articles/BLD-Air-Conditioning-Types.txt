<span class="winner-kicker">Best system type for most homes</span><h3>Start with a correctly sized reverse-cycle split</h3>
<p>This complete 5.0/6.0 kW kit illustrates the main-zone route, but its capacity is right only where an installer’s room-load calculation supports it. A separate smaller split can serve an occupied bedroom more effectively than oversizing the lounge unit.</p>
</div>
</div>

<div class="intro-card fancy-card">
<p>For most Australian homes and apartments, an efficient reverse-cycle split-system air conditioner is the best starting point. It cools in summer, heats in winter, lets you condition only the rooms being used and usually costs less to install than ducted air conditioning. The correct answer still depends on the floor plan, room size, insulation, windows, climate, outdoor-unit location and strata or rental restrictions.</p>

<div class="callout pick">
<strong>Quick answer:</strong> Choose one correctly sized reverse-cycle split system for the main living area if you want the best balance of purchase price, efficiency and control. Add a smaller separate split to a frequently used bedroom if needed. Consider a multi-split when outdoor-unit space is restricted, and ducted reverse cycle when you own a suitable house, want whole-home comfort and will use effective zoning. Treat a portable air conditioner as a temporary compromise rather than the default.</div>

<div class="callout warning">
<strong>Do not choose capacity from floor area alone.</strong> Ceiling height, glass, orientation, shading, insulation, air leakage, climate and adjoining spaces can materially change the load. Ask a competent installer to complete a room-by-room heat-load calculation before accepting a quote.</div>

<h2>Best choice for most apartments</h2>

<p>A reverse-cycle split system is usually the strongest apartment choice when approval and outdoor-unit placement are available. A unit in the living area can cool the room people use most without paying to condition the whole apartment. A separate bedroom system provides better overnight control than trying to push living-room air down a hallway.</p>

<p>Do not expect one large split in the lounge to cool closed bedrooms evenly. Air does not turn corners or pass through doorways reliably, and the thermostat may reach its target while distant rooms remain uncomfortable.</p>

<p>Before buying, confirm:</p>

<ul>
<li>written landlord approval if you rent;</li>
<li>the strata scheme’s renovation process and by-laws;</li>
<li>whether walls, balconies, ceilings or service ducts are common property;</li>
<li>where condensate water can legally drain;</li>
<li>the outdoor unit’s permitted position and mounting method;</li>
<li>noise effects on neighbouring bedrooms and balconies;</li>
<li>electrical capacity and access to an appropriate circuit; and</li>
<li>how pipes and cables will cross fire-rated or waterproofed building elements.</li>
</ul>

<p>In NSW, installing or replacing a reverse-cycle air conditioner can fall within strata minor-renovation rules, but work affecting the exterior, structure or other common property may require a different approval pathway. The individual scheme’s by-laws and proposal details matter, so obtain approval before paying a deposit.</p>


<div class="callout warning">
<strong>Apartment trap:</strong> The outdoor condenser being physically able to fit on the balcony does not mean it is approved. Clearance, airflow, vibration, noise, drainage, appearance and common-property rules must all be resolved.</div>



<h2>Best choice for a small house or townhouse</h2>

<p>One split system in the main living zone plus separate systems in regularly occupied bedrooms is often more economical and controllable than a small ducted system. You can run only the area being used and avoid duct losses through a hot roof space.</p>

<p>This arrangement suits households whose occupants keep different schedules. Someone working from home can condition one room during the day without cooling every bedroom. At night, the living-area system can remain off while a quiet bedroom unit operates at a modest setting.</p>

<p>The trade-off is having several indoor and outdoor units. A multi-split may reduce the number of condensers, but it is not automatically cheaper or more efficient. Compare installed cost, rated efficiency when several heads operate, pipe lengths, service access and what happens to all rooms if the shared outdoor unit fails.</p>



<h2>Best choice for a larger house</h2>

<p>Ducted reverse-cycle air conditioning can suit a large home when appearance, convenience and even distribution matter. It works best with thoughtful zoning, adequate return-air paths, sealed and insulated ducts, and controls that prevent the system from trying to condition too little an area for its operating range.</p>

<p>Ask the designer to explain:</p>

<ul>
<li>the heat-load calculation for each room;</li>
<li>the supply-air quantity and outlet position;</li>
<li>where return air will travel when bedroom doors are closed;</li>
<li>how small each active zone is allowed to become;</li>
<li>duct insulation, sealing and routing;</li>
<li>indoor and outdoor unit service access;</li>
<li>condensate drainage and overflow protection; and</li>
<li>expected electrical demand when several zones operate.</li>
</ul>

<p>A badly designed ducted system can be noisy, inefficient and uneven. More zones do not repair undersized ducts, poor air balance or an incorrectly selected central unit.</p>

<h2>Why bigger is not always better</h2>

<p>An oversized unit may cool the air quickly and stop before removing enough moisture, particularly in humid areas. It may cycle frequently, create temperature swings and cost more to buy. An undersized unit may run continuously during extreme weather, struggle to reach the set temperature and provide poor comfort.</p>

<p>Modern inverter systems vary compressor output, which improves part-load operation, but sizing still matters. Ask for the unit’s minimum and maximum output—not only its headline capacity—especially for a bedroom or highly insulated room with a small overnight load.</p>



<h2>Reverse cycle is usually the sensible default</h2>

<p>A reverse-cycle air conditioner is an air-source heat pump. It moves heat rather than creating all warmth through electrical resistance. The Australian Government’s Your Home guidance describes reverse-cycle air conditioners as highly efficient for both heating and cooling.</p>

<p>Cooling-only models may cost less initially, but they remove the option of efficient winter heating. Unless the room will never need heat, compare the small upfront saving with the value of year-round use.</p>



<h2>When evaporative cooling makes sense</h2>

<p>Evaporative cooling works best in dry inland climates. It introduces cooled outside air and normally requires windows or doors to remain partly open so warm air can escape. It is less effective as humidity rises.</p>

<p>Your Home notes that evaporative systems consume water, can suffer mineral build-up in hard-water areas and may bring outside smoke into the home during bushfires. High-efficiency reverse-cycle air conditioning is generally more effective and efficient across a wider range of conditions.</p>

<p>Do not choose evaporative cooling solely because its electrical use appears low. Consider local humidity, water availability, maintenance, smoke, security with open windows and winter heat leakage through roof ducting.</p>



<h2>When a portable air conditioner is acceptable</h2>

<p>A portable refrigerated air conditioner may be reasonable for a renter who cannot install anything permanent or for occasional emergency cooling. It must exhaust hot air outside through a properly sealed window kit.</p>

<p>Single-hose units pull room air across the condenser and expel it outdoors, which can draw hot outside air back through gaps elsewhere. They are also noisy because the compressor sits inside the room. A portable evaporative cooler without an exhaust hose is not a refrigerated air conditioner and will not cool a closed room in the same way.</p>


<div class="callout">
<strong>Portable-unit reality:</strong> If permanent installation is possible, a correctly sized split system will normally be quieter, more efficient and more effective. Buy portable only when the installation constraint is genuine.</div>



<h2>How to compare energy efficiency</h2>

<p>Use the Zoned Energy Rating Label rather than comparing star counts without context. The label can show different performance for hot, average and cold climate zones, because a system’s suitability changes with outdoor conditions.</p>

<p>Compare models with similar rated capacity and examine:</p>

<ul>
<li>the star rating for your climate zone;</li>
<li>annual energy-consumption estimates on the label;</li>
<li>cooling and heating efficiency;</li>
<li>minimum operating output;</li>
<li>standby consumption;</li>
<li>indoor and outdoor sound-power figures; and</li>
<li>performance at high and low outdoor temperatures.</li>
</ul>

<p>A high-efficiency unit cannot compensate for an unshaded western window, open door or uninsulated ceiling. Improving the building can reduce the capacity required and lower bills for the life of the system.</p>



<h2>Noise matters more in an apartment</h2>

<p>Indoor noise affects sleep and television listening. Outdoor noise and vibration can affect neighbours and trigger complaints. Do not compare only the quietest indoor fan setting; review the outdoor-unit sound data and likely operation during hot nights.</p>

<p>The condenser needs adequate airflow and should not discharge hot air into a confined balcony corner. Avoid mounting it next to a neighbour’s bedroom where practical. Correct brackets, vibration isolation and a rigid mounting surface help, but must suit the building and manufacturer’s instructions.</p>



<h2>Installation quality can matter more than brand</h2>

<p>A good unit can perform poorly after bad installation. Refrigerant pipe length, pipe insulation, condensate fall, electrical supply, wall penetration sealing, outdoor clearances, commissioning and drainage all matter.</p>

<p>Obtain an itemised quote stating:</p>

<ul>
<li>exact indoor and outdoor model numbers;</li>
<li>cooling and heating capacity;</li>
<li>pipe and cable allowance;</li>
<li>mounting brackets or slab;</li>
<li>condensate route and pump if required;</li>
<li>switchboard or circuit work;</li>
<li>removal and lawful disposal of an old system;</li>
<li>warranty responsibilities; and</li>
<li>all exclusions and possible variations.</li>
</ul>

<p>Refrigerant work must be handled by appropriately licensed people. Check the relevant state electrical or contractor licence and the ARCtick refrigerant authorisation that applies to the work.</p>



<h2>Features worth paying for</h2>

<ul>
<li>
<strong>Good part-load efficiency:</strong> The system spends much of its life below maximum output.</li>
<li>
<strong>Low indoor noise:</strong> Particularly important for bedrooms and apartments.</li>
<li>
<strong>Wide airflow control:</strong> Helps prevent cold air blowing directly onto seating or beds.</li>
<li>
<strong>Useful timer and scheduling:</strong> Reduces unnecessary operation.</li>
<li>
<strong>Demand-response capability:</strong> May suit some electricity plans or incentive programs.</li>
<li>
<strong>Accessible washable filters:</strong> Maintenance is more likely to be completed.</li>
<li>
<strong>Strong local service support:</strong> Parts and competent technicians matter after the warranty claim begins.</li>
</ul>

<p>Wi-Fi control can be convenient, but it should not outweigh efficiency, quietness, support and correct sizing. Check whether essential controls still work without an account, cloud connection or phone app.</p>



<h2>Reduce the load before buying a larger unit</h2>

<ol>
<li>Shade west- and north-facing glass appropriately.</li>
<li>Use curtains or blinds with minimal gaps.</li>
<li>Seal obvious air leakage around doors and windows.</li>
<li>Improve ceiling insulation where practical.</li>
<li>Use ceiling fans to increase comfort at a higher thermostat setting.</li>
<li>Close off unused areas when the system design permits it.</li>
<li>Reduce oven and appliance heat during the hottest period.</li>
</ol>

<p>A fan does not lower room temperature, but moving air can make occupants feel cooler while using far less electricity than compressor cooling.</p>



<h2>Mistakes to avoid</h2>

<ul>
<li>
<strong>Buying by floor area alone:</strong> Insulation, glass and orientation can change the required capacity substantially.</li>
<li>
<strong>Automatically choosing the largest unit:</strong> Oversizing can reduce comfort and efficiency.</li>
<li>
<strong>Expecting one split to cool closed bedrooms:</strong> Condition occupied rooms directly.</li>
<li>
<strong>Installing before strata approval:</strong> You may be required to remove or relocate the system.</li>
<li>
<strong>Ignoring outdoor noise:</strong> The neighbour hears the condenser, not your quiet indoor fan.</li>
<li>
<strong>Choosing ducted without zoning:</strong> Whole-home operation can waste energy when only one room is occupied.</li>
<li>
<strong>Comparing star ratings from different capacities:</strong> Use the full Zoned Energy Rating Label and compare like with like.</li>
<li>
<strong>Buying the cheapest installation:</strong> Missing electrical, drainage or pipe work can create expensive variations.</li>
<li>
<strong>Blocking airflow:</strong> Furniture, curtains and confined condenser locations reduce performance.</li>
<li>
<strong>Neglecting filters:</strong> Restricted airflow hurts comfort and efficiency.</li>
</ul>



<p>In NSW, ask a participating installer to confirm eligibility and include any Energy Savings Scheme discount in the quote. Complete the required nomination paperwork <strong>before installation begins</strong>; the scheme does not reimburse work already completed. Not every model qualifies.</p>
<h2>A practical buying process</h2>

<ol>
<li><strong>Choose the rooms that genuinely need conditioning.</strong></li>
<li><strong>Measure each room, ceiling height, windows and connected spaces.</strong></li>
<li><strong>Improve obvious shading, sealing and insulation weaknesses.</strong></li>
<li><strong>Resolve landlord, strata and planning requirements.</strong></li>
<li><strong>Obtain at least two itemised installation proposals.</strong></li>
<li><strong>Ask each installer to explain the heat-load calculation.</strong></li>
<li><strong>Compare climate-zone efficiency, noise and local support.</strong></li>
<li><strong>Confirm outdoor placement, drainage and electrical work.</strong></li>
<li><strong>Check available state incentives before accepting the quote.</strong></li>
<li><strong>Keep invoices, approvals, model numbers and commissioning records.</strong></li>
</ol>

<h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>


<h3>What type of air conditioner is best for an apartment?</h3>

<p>A correctly sized reverse-cycle split system is usually best when strata, landlord and outdoor-unit placement requirements can be satisfied. It is efficient, quiet indoors and independently controlled.</p>


<h3>Is ducted air conditioning worth it?</h3>

<p>It can be in a larger owner-occupied house where discreet whole-home comfort matters. Good zoning, duct design and return-air paths are essential. Multiple split systems may cost less to run when only a few rooms are occupied.</p>


<h3>Can one split system cool an entire apartment?</h3>

<p>It may cool a compact open-plan apartment with good airflow, but closed bedrooms and rooms around corners will often remain warmer. Do not oversize the lounge unit to compensate for poor air distribution.</p>


<h3>What temperature should I set?</h3>

<p>Use the highest cooling setting that remains comfortable and the lowest comfortable heating setting. Small thermostat changes can materially affect energy use. Fans, shading and suitable clothing can widen the comfortable range.</p>


<h3>Should I leave the air conditioner running all day?</h3>

<p>Usually not when the home is empty for a long period. Efficient pre-cooling, sensible scheduling and reducing heat entry can help. Exceptions may apply for health, pets, humidity control or unusually extreme conditions.</p>


<h3>Do I need a 2.5kW or 3.5kW unit for a bedroom?</h3>

<p>Many ordinary bedrooms fall near those sizes, but glass, orientation, ceiling height, insulation and climate determine the answer. A proper calculation is safer than using room area alone.</p>



<div class="verdict">
<h2>Final verdict</h2>
<strong>Verdict:</strong> For most Australian apartments, townhouses and ordinary homes, buy a correctly sized reverse-cycle split system for each important occupied zone. Use a multi-split only when limited outdoor space justifies its extra complexity. Choose ducted reverse cycle for a larger suitable house when whole-home comfort and appearance are worth the higher cost—and insist on proper zoning and design. Use portable air conditioning only when permanent installation is genuinely unavailable.</div>
